Lesson 1 · From errors to a hypothesis

Combat helps explain whether. Geometry helps explain where.

V0 predicts three final outcomes from the current state: A plant, B plant, or no plant. Its 683 mistakes contain two different problems: 575 cross the Plant / No Plant boundary (84.2%), while 108 confuse A and B (15.8%). This motivates asking whether one three-class task contains two useful subproblems.

Will a plant happen?

The corrected diagnostic depends most strongly on combat information. Alive players, health and armor help describe whether the round can still reach a plant.

If it does, which site?

Among rounds that really planted, offensive geometry dominates the A-versus-B diagnostic. Positions help distinguish where the plant will occur.

Permutation importance measures how much a score worsens after a feature group is shuffled. It describes the fitted model’s reliance on that information; it does not prove a causal effect. A diagnostic restricted to true plant outcomes is useful for analysis, but that future outcome cannot be supplied to a live predictor.

Two probabilities, three outcomes

Let q be the probability of any plant, and r the probability of A conditional on a plant. The Plant Head learns from all training rows. The Site Head learns only from training rows with an actual A or B plant. At prediction time, both heads use the available state; no future label is needed.

P(A)=qr,P(B)=q(1−r),P(NO)=1−q

Example: q = 0.80 and r = 0.75 produce A = 0.60, B = 0.20 and NO = 0.20. These probabilities sum to one.

This structure is an inductive bias: a proposed organization of the prediction problem. It makes tactical sense, but only an experiment can establish whether it improves forecasts.

Lesson 2 · Change one experimental factor at a time

H0 tests the structure. H1 specializes. H2 calibrates.

  1. H0 — hierarchy alone. Keep the corrected dataset, frozen match folds, 37 inputs per head and XGBoost configuration. Change the flat classifier into two binary heads. Full log loss rises to 0.852546, against V0’s 0.838182. This particular hierarchy fails to replace V0; it does not disprove every hierarchical model.
  2. H1 — specialize the inputs. Use 36 inputs for the Plant Head and 27 for the Site Head, excluding combat and horizon from the latter. Site log loss improves from H0’s 0.501147 to 0.478017. Full log loss reaches 0.840649, still above V0.
  3. H2 — calibrate only the Site Head. Preserve the H1 predictor and fit a probability correction using nested, match-grouped training predictions. Site log loss improves to 0.471829; full log loss becomes 0.837397. The next question is whether that small gain holds across matches.

A loss decomposition locates the bottleneck

LLfull=LLplant+nplantNLLsite

For H1: 0.589449 + (886 / 1686) × 0.478017 ≈ 0.840649.

Every row contributes a plant loss; only the 886 actual plant rows contribute a conditional site loss. This identity follows from taking the negative logarithm of the composed probabilities, using the same rows and probability definitions. It does not apply unchanged to Brier or F1.

H2’s conditional site accuracy stays at 0.758465 while site log loss and Brier improve. Probability quality can improve without changing aggregate accuracy. Equal accuracy alone does not establish that every individual predicted class stayed identical.

The second branch: does longer history earn more complexity?

T0 separately tests temporal information with the existing XGBoost approach: 37 current features plus 34 changes over 3 and 5 seconds, giving 71 inputs. It preserves the 1,686-row population. One 5-second history lookup needs the documented one-tick fallback; it must not silently remove a row.

Lesson 4 · Evaluation machinery

No leakage does not mean no optimism

Leakage means information that should be unavailable enters training or evaluation. Examples include future-state features, putting rows from the same match on both sides of a train/test split, or fitting a calibrator using outer-test labels.

Optimism is different. An evaluation can still look better than future performance because model decisions repeatedly adapt to the same development evidence. H0, H1, H2 and T0 all reuse the same 20-match corpus. Their evaluation can therefore be leakage-safe while still being development evidence.

Nested calibration

Inside each outer training partition, inner grouped folds generate held-out Site Head probabilities. The calibrator is fitted only from those inner OOF predictions and labels.

What nesting protects

Outer-test labels never train the base model or calibration parameters. This prevents calibration leakage. It cannot erase the fact that H2 was designed after observing H0 and H1.

Platt calibration

H2 converts the raw Site probability p to log-odds, learns a linear correction, then converts the result back to a probability. 

z = ln(p / (1 - p))

p_cal = sigmoid(a * z + b)

The fitted H2 slopes are roughly 0.71–0.78. A slope below one compresses log-odds variation, which is consistent with a raw Site Head that is somewhat too sharp. The intercept also shifts the odds, so this is not simply a mechanical move of every probability toward 0.5.

Paired match bootstrap

For each match we define ΔLL = LL(H2) - LL(V0). Negative means H2 is better. We resample the 20 match-level differences with replacement, compute the equal-match mean, and repeat this 100,000 times.

The mean can be pulled by a minority of larger gains or losses. The median describes the middle observed match. This is why H2 can have a negative mean delta while its median delta is positive.

Key distinction: nested calibration makes the H2 evaluation leakage-safe. It does not make H2 independent confirmatory evidence, because the same 20 matches informed the research path that produced H2.

Development set versus holdout set

A development set is allowed to influence features, architectures, diagnostics and model selection. A holdout set is reserved before experimentation and should not influence those choices.

Grouped cross-validation can still be used inside the development set. The untouched holdout answers a different question: does the frozen candidate reproduce its advantage on genuinely new matches?

Preregistration

Before opening the future holdout, V2 should commit a protocol specifying the candidate, frozen baseline, primary metrics, grouping unit, comparison method and promotion gate. This prevents changing the success rule after seeing the result.

Multiple comparisons

Trying many feature subsets, calibration methods, hyperparameters and temporal windows on the same development corpus increases the probability that one model looks unusually good because of sample noise. V2 should therefore make each experiment answer one explicit hypothesis instead of performing an open-ended search for the lowest score.

More matches matter more than simply more rows

The 1,686 V0 observations are not 1,686 independent match experiences. Rows from a match share players, strategy and context. Increasing the diversity of independent matches is therefore more valuable for confirmation than sampling the same matches more densely.

H2's next role

H2 becomes a promising frozen hypothesis. On new independent data, the cleanest test is to evaluate the existing H2 design before retuning it.

Sequence-model gate

A GRU, LSTM, TCN or Transformer should reopen only when new evidence identifies useful path-dependent information that snapshots or simple deltas cannot represent, or when substantially more data supports a sequence model.

Current V2 principle: the scarce resource is not model complexity. It is new, independent, informative evidence.
Reference · What it means, why we use it, how to read it

Fourteen concepts behind the decision

01 · Log Loss (LL)

Log loss measures the probability assigned to the true class. For one observation it is −ln(p of the true outcome); the reported score averages this over observations. Lower is better. Natural logarithms give losses of about 0.223, 2.303 and 4.605 when the true class receives 0.80, 0.10 and 0.01 respectively.

It strongly penalizes confident mistakes. Accuracy only checks the highest-probability class, so it cannot distinguish a correct 51% forecast from a correct 95% forecast. Our probability forecast needs both the outcome and its confidence to be useful; LL is the primary metric.

02 · Brier Score

Brier measures squared probability error. For a true A outcome and prediction [0.8, 0.1, 0.1], the three-class score is (0.8 − 1)² + 0.1² + 0.1² = 0.06. Average this sum over rows; lower is better.

The project uses the sum across all three classes, without dividing by three. Binary head Brier instead uses (p − y)² for one positive-class probability. Those scales differ. Unlike log loss, Brier is bounded and does not grow without limit when the true class receives almost zero probability.

03 · Out-of-fold (OOF) prediction

A prediction made while that observation’s entire match is held out of training. Repeat across the five frozen folds until every row has a held-out prediction. Pooling combines all 1,686 predictions to calculate one score. This evaluates held-out forecasts, rather than a model’s ability to reproduce its training examples.

04 · Grouped cross-validation (CV)

A fold is one partition used for evaluation. Grouping keeps every row from a match in the same fold: one fold is tested while the others train the model. Rows and horizons from a match share context and are correlated; randomly splitting rows would let related match information appear on both sides. The frozen grouping also makes V0 and V1 comparisons use the same held-out matches.

05 · Calibration

Calibration asks whether forecast probabilities agree with observed frequencies. Among many comparable 70% forecasts, roughly 70% should occur. A model can rank classes well while overstating its confidence. Correcting that confidence can improve LL or Brier without increasing accuracy; these probability scores also reflect properties beyond calibration alone.

06 · Platt calibration

In H2, convert the raw site probability p to log-odds z, then fit a logistic correction. Probabilities are clipped away from zero and one before the logarithm.

z=ln(p1−p),pcal=11+e−(az+b)

The fitted slope a changes confidence and b shifts the odds. H2 slopes are about 0.71–0.78, compressing log-odds variation. Because the intercept also matters, this does not mean every probability simply moves toward 0.5. H2 applies this only to the Site Head.

07 · Nested calibration

The calibrator learns from labels, so it needs its own separation. Within each outer training partition, inner grouped folds produce held-out site probabilities. Fit the calibrator on those inner OOF predictions and labels; refit the base head on outer training data; then apply the head and calibrator to the untouched outer test matches.

Outer train → inner grouped OOF → fit calibration → refit base head → predict outer test → calibrate. The outer test labels never fit the base head or the calibration parameters.

08 · Leakage

Leakage occurs when information unavailable to a legitimate prediction or training process enters it. Examples include post-outcome snapshot features, splitting one match between training and test, or fitting a calibrator on the outer test labels. Using plant labels to select Site Head training rows is legitimate supervised training; using the future plant label to route a live prediction would not be.

09 · Optimism

Optimism means an evaluation can look better than future performance because development decisions adapted to the same evidence. H0, H1 and H2 reused the same 20 development matches. Even with correctly held-out training predictions, repeatedly choosing features or methods after seeing scores makes this a development comparison, not an untouched final test.

Nested calibration prevents calibration-fitting leakage. It does not erase the broader history of research choices. New independent matches are needed to assess generalization beyond that history.

10 · Per-match robustness

Pooled LL weights each observation equally, so matches contributing more eligible rows count more. Per-match analysis computes each model’s loss on each match first. The difference Δ = LL(H2) − LL(V0) is negative when H2 improves. An equal-match mean then gives every match one vote in the average; counting wins asks yet another question and ignores the size of each win or loss.

11 · Paired match bootstrap

Take the 20 observed match differences. Sample 20 matches with replacement, preserving each H2–V0 pair, and calculate the mean difference. Repeat 100,000 times. A match can appear multiple times or not at all. Resampling matches preserves within-match dependence better than treating every row as independent.

This estimates sensitivity to the mix of observed matches. It does not create new matches or measure every source of model-training or domain-shift uncertainty.

12 · A 95% bootstrap interval

The reported percentile interval takes the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap mean differences. H2’s LL interval crosses zero. The data therefore do not show a stable directional advantage under this analysis; crossing zero does not prove equivalence.

The fraction of bootstrap means below zero is 0.5918 for LL and 0.8621 for Brier. These are resampling summaries, not p-values and not a 59.18% or 86.21% probability that H2 is truly better. Only 20 development matches support this estimate.

13 · Mean versus median

The mean adds all 20 differences and divides by 20; large improvements or regressions can pull it. The median sorts them and averages the two middle values. H2’s mean is −0.001852, but its median is +0.007943. These can coexist: the magnitude of a minority of gains can outweigh more numerous small losses. Neither statistic alone describes the whole distribution.

14 · Why no GRU?

A GRU is a recurrent neural network that learns a state across a sequence. It adds modeling and tuning complexity. V1 first asked whether simple longer-history features improve the fixed baseline. T0 failed that gate, so there is no evidence here to justify escalating to a sequence model. Future work can revisit temporal modeling with a new representation, more data and a fresh evaluation plan.
